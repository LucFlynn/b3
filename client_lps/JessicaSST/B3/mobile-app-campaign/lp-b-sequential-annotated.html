Four numbers, at a glance, before anyone reads a word

People don't read landing pages, they scan them — eye-tracking work at Nielsen Norman Group puts it at roughly 20–28% of the words on the page. So the four facts that establish you're real go somewhere they can be absorbed in about two seconds, at the top, in large type.

All four are answers to unspoken objections: are you big enough (30+), have you done this before (100+), will your people stick around (90%), what happens after go-live (1 year).

You've heard the story. You may have lived it.

A project starts at six months and a number everyone can live with. Then a requirement surfaces that wasn't in scope. Then the lead developer rolls off to another account. Then the timeline moves, and moves again.

Eighteen months later you've spent triple, the software does most of what you asked for, and nobody in operations wants to use it.

That outcome isn't bad luck. It's what happens when the vendor bills for hours instead of committing to a result. Hourly billing means every delay is revenue for them and cost for you. The incentives point in opposite directions from the day you sign.

The “not for you” paragraph is not a mistake

Turning people away on the page is counterintuitive, and it's one of the more reliable moves in lead generation. Right now about 31% of your identified ad spend is going to people who will never buy from you — developers registering apps in the Spotify dashboard, people searching how to build an app themselves, and individuals with an app idea and a few thousand dollars. Some of that gets fixed in the ad account with negative keywords. The rest gets fixed here, by saying plainly that you're not the low bidder and you don't build consumer apps on spec.

The five bullets above it are written as symptoms, not services — "your team still moves data between systems by hand" rather than "systems integration." A buyer recognizes their own situation faster than they recognize a category name. The last bullet, needing a defensible number for a board or a CFO, is aimed straight at the person who has to justify this internally.

What changed in the form, and why each change matters

  • The budget dropdown is gone entirely. On the current page it's required, and the first option is "Under $25,000." That asks a stranger to disclose their number before you've given them anything, and it frames the conversation around your revenue rather than their problem.
  • The CAPTCHA is gone. It rejects real people at a measurable rate. Spam gets handled invisibly on the back end instead.
  • Four fields, and four is the ceiling. The steep drop-off in published form data sits between four and seven.
  • "Four fields" is stated above the form so the visitor knows the ask is small before deciding whether to start. How long a form looks matters as much as how long it is.
  • The phone stays available. Some buyers at this size will never fill in a form, and a call is a better lead anyway.

Once live, we can see form starts, form completions, and calls as three separate numbers — so if people are starting the form and abandoning it, we'll know, rather than guessing.

The FAQ exists to answer price without publishing a price

You've told me you won't publish a price range, and I agree with that — a range that doesn't apply to the reader's project does more harm than silence. But "what does this cost" is the first question in everyone's head, and a page that ignores it entirely loses the reader.

So the FAQ answers it honestly: it depends on scope, which is why we scope before quoting, and you'll have a real number in writing before committing to anything. That turns a refusal into a demonstration of the method.

The other four questions are the objections that actually kill these deals — how can you fix-price it, what if we change our minds, who writes the code, do we have to rip out what we already run. Answering them on the page means they don't have to become a phone call before someone will raise their hand.
